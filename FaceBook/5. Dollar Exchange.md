Tuesdays bring their own challenges. Where do I exchange dollars in rural Barbosa?

Of course, I do it in a coffee depot. Local farmers bring their coffee beans to centralized locations. Some beans require extra drying, so they are dumped on the streets and walkways. By the way, locals drink mostly water. I say that the majority is in a permanent state of hypermania, and some extra caffeine would drive them into unhealthy mania. Natural selection eliminates risky locals who dare to try coffee.:)

Personally, I avoid coffee and tea due to the presence of caffeine, which is a mild narcotic officially classified as a psychoactive stimulant. If I am not mistaken, caffeine has some overlapping downstream dopamine circuitry in the brain with cocaine. An interesting fact is that, originally, Britons drank coffee and tea. And Sri Lanka was the world's largest coffee-producing colony, growing this recreational aromatic drug. But around 1870, a tropical fungus, coffee leaf rust (*Hemileia vastatrix*), discovered coffee as well and consumed all coffee plantations. So, out of pity for narco withdrawal, Britons completely switched to tea.

This fungus eventually reached Colombia and could have wiped out its coffee production. But Colombia began breeding rust-resistant coffee in advance. However, the fungus stays optimistic and is constantly evolving to overcome resistance. It is very possible that the day will come when Colombia will be coffee-free. Of course, out of pity for narco withdrawal, now the U.S. will legalize coca leaves as a substitute drink of choice. Coca leaves are already legalized for infusions and as an herbal tea, mate de coca, in Bolivia. I have seen in a movie their coca leaf depot, and it was indistinguishable from the coffee depot in Barbosa. Once the fungus eradicates coffee in Colombia, it will be an easy and smooth switch to coca agriculture. Finally, Coca-Cola will have coke again.
